# OCS Taxonomy Governance Policy

## 1. Purpose

The OCS taxonomy is intended to provide a coherent, extensible, and broadly applicable classification of domains. Its structure should maximize the usefulness of domain groupings while minimizing unnecessary fragmentation, redundant classifications, and the proliferation of categories created for individual use cases.

Taxonomy decisions shall prioritize conceptual coherence, broad applicability, and consistent classification over exhaustive enumeration or accommodation of every conceivable distinction.

## 2. Inductive Classification

The OCS taxonomy shall be developed **inductively rather than deductively**.

Categories should emerge from the identification and grouping of meaningful domains, recurring concepts, and recognizable relationships. The taxonomy should evolve from observed classification needs rather than from an attempt to enumerate every possible concept or logically derive a category for every conceivable subject.

The purpose of categorization is to identify useful groupings of related domains, not to provide a separate category for every idea, application, or use case.

Accordingly:

- **Generalize before specializing.** Prefer existing, broadly applicable categories over narrowly tailored subdivisions.
- **Group by meaningful relationships.** Categories should reflect substantive similarities among the domains they contain.
- **Avoid unnecessary proliferation.** A concept's distinct identity does not, by itself, justify the creation of a new category.
- **Prioritize reusable classifications.** Categories should accommodate multiple related subjects and use cases wherever appropriate.
- **Refine through application.** New categories should emerge when experience demonstrates a meaningful classification need that existing categories cannot adequately satisfy.

The burden of justification rests on the creation of a new category, not on the continued use of an existing one.

## 3. Category Creation and Revision

Before creating, splitting, merging, or relocating a category, the decision-maker shall evaluate whether the proposed change improves the taxonomy as a whole.

The following criteria shall guide that evaluation:

1. **Conceptual coherence:** Does the proposed category represent a meaningful grouping of related domains?
2. **Distinctiveness:** Is the proposed grouping materially different from existing categories?
3. **General applicability:** Can the category serve multiple related subjects or use cases rather than a single narrowly defined application?
4. **Necessity:** Can the subject be classified adequately within an existing category?
5. **Structural fit:** Does the proposed placement preserve the taxonomy's established hierarchy and naming conventions?
6. **Net benefit:** Does the improvement in clarity and usefulness justify the additional structural complexity?

A new category should be introduced only when existing categories cannot adequately represent the subject without compromising the taxonomy's coherence or practical usefulness.

New top-level categories require particular scrutiny. The interdisciplinary nature, broad relevance, or apparent uniqueness of a subject does not, by itself, justify creating a new top-level domain.

Where an existing category can reasonably accommodate a proposed subject, that category should be preferred over the creation of a new one.

## 4. Exclusive Domain Classification

Each domain shall be assigned to **the single category most appropriate to its particular subject matter**.

Classification shall be based on the domain's primary subject, defining purpose, and substantive focus—not merely on its associations with other domains or the number of contexts in which it can be applied.

The following rules apply:

- **One primary category per domain.** A domain shall not be duplicated across multiple categories simply because it is relevant to several subjects.
- **Classify by primary subject matter.** The assigned category should best represent what the domain fundamentally concerns.
- **Distinguish subject matter from application.** A domain should not be moved into a category merely because it is used in that category's context.
- **Prefer the best existing fit.** When several categories appear plausible, select the single category that most directly represents the domain's defining subject matter.
- **Avoid duplicative exceptions.** Cross-domain relevance does not justify multiple primary classifications.

For example, digital accessibility may be relevant to law, public policy, social participation, and software engineering. Its primary classification should nevertheless be determined by the specific subject matter of the domain being classified. A domain concerned with legal accessibility obligations belongs with the most appropriate legal classification; a domain concerned with implementing accessible software belongs with the most appropriate software classification. These are distinct domains, not grounds for duplicating one domain across multiple categories.

Where the taxonomy supports references or relationships between categories, those mechanisms may be used to express secondary associations without compromising exclusive primary classification.

## 5. Domain Rating System

Each domain shall receive a rating from **1 to 10 within its assigned category**. The rating is calculated by adding two component scores:

**Domain Rating = Cultural Relevance Score + Author Authority Score**

Each component is scored on a scale from 1 to 5, inclusive. The scores are additive and shall not be averaged or weighted.

### 5.1 Cultural Relevance Score

The cultural relevance score measures the relevance and significance of a domain within the cultural context for which the taxonomy is being maintained.

The assessment should consider the domain's recognition, practical importance, social significance, and relevance to the practices or concerns of the applicable culture or community.

| Score | General interpretation |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Minimal cultural relevance |
| 2 | Limited cultural relevance |
| 3 | Moderate cultural relevance |
| 4 | High cultural relevance |
| 5 | Very high cultural relevance |

Cultural relevance shall be assessed in context. A domain's relevance may differ between cultures, communities, and periods; it should not be assumed to be universal.

### 5.2 Author Authority Score

The author authority score measures the authority attributed to the author responsible for establishing, describing, or contributing the domain.

The assessment should consider relevant subject-matter expertise, demonstrated competence, credibility, and standing in the domain concerned.

| Score | General interpretation |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Minimal relevant authority |
| 2 | Limited relevant authority |
| 3 | Moderate relevant authority |
| 4 | High relevant authority |
| 5 | Very high relevant authority |

Authority shall be evaluated relative to the subject matter. Recognition or expertise in an unrelated field shall not automatically confer authority within the domain being rated.

### 5.3 Calculation and Interpretation

The cultural relevance score and author authority score shall be added to determine the domain's final rating.

| Cultural relevance | Author authority | Final rating |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 1 | 2 |
| 2 | 3 | 5 |
| 3 | 3 | 6 |
| 4 | 4 | 8 |
| 5 | 5 | 10 |

Both component scores shall be retained alongside the final rating to preserve transparency and explain how the result was obtained.

The rating is a combined assessment of cultural relevance and author authority. It shall not be interpreted as a direct measure of objective truth, intrinsic worth, or universal importance.

The rating shall not determine a domain's category. Classification determines where a domain belongs; rating evaluates the domain according to the two specified criteria.

**Scale clarification:** Although the rating system is described as a 1–10 scale, the specified formula produces values from 2 to 10 because both component scores have a minimum of 1. A rating of 1 is therefore unattainable unless the scoring formula or component ranges are revised.

## 6. Consistency and Review

Taxonomy decisions and domain ratings should be applied consistently across categories.

When a classification is disputed, the decision-maker should compare the candidate categories and select the one that best fits the domain's primary subject matter. The existence of secondary relationships should not override the requirement for a single primary category.

When a category change is proposed, the rationale should identify the deficiency in the existing structure and explain how the change improves the taxonomy. Revisions should preserve established conventions wherever possible and avoid introducing unnecessary complexity.

Domain ratings should be reassessed when there are material changes in cultural relevance, author authority, or the evidence supporting the original assessment.

Where reasonable disagreement exists, the rationale for the classification or rating should be documented so that subsequent decisions can be evaluated against the same principles.

## 7. Governing Principles

All taxonomy decisions shall be guided by the following principles:

- **Induction over exhaustive deduction:** Categories emerge from meaningful groupings rather than from the enumeration of every conceivable subject.
- **Generalization over proliferation:** Existing categories should be preferred when they adequately represent a domain.
- **Exclusive primary classification:** Each domain belongs to the single category that best fits its defining subject matter.
- **Conceptual coherence over convenience:** Structural decisions should improve the taxonomy as a whole rather than satisfy isolated use cases.
- **Consistency over arbitrary exceptions:** Established classification rules should be applied uniformly.
- **Transparency in evaluation:** Domain ratings shall preserve both component scores and their calculated total.
- **Evolution through demonstrated need:** The taxonomy may change as practical use reveals meaningful gaps or opportunities for improvement.

## 8. Final Decision Standard

A taxonomy decision is justified when it improves the coherence, usability, or general applicability of the classification system without introducing unnecessary complexity.

The preferred taxonomy is the simplest structure that adequately represents meaningful domains, assigns each domain to its single best-fitting category, and supports a broad range of use cases without requiring a new category for every distinct idea.

The taxonomy shall expand in response to demonstrated classification needs and meaningful conceptual distinctions—not merely because a new subject, application, or use case can be identified.
